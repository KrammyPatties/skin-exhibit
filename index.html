What is your race?

…and who gets to decide?

Three mixed-race Singaporeans. One question they have been asked all their lives.

  1. Read each person's first glance: what a stranger might see, hear and notice.
  2. What race would you assume, and why? Write it on your paper sheet, or in the box.
  3. Reveal their story, in their own words.
  4. At the end, turn your sheet over to reflect.

The first-glance cards show only what some people notice and assume. Try it, then see what it misses. Names changed or used with permission. No photos. Quotes lightly trimmed; … marks words left out.

Why I'm asking

I'm read as Malay. I'm Indian.

I'm Indian Muslim: 7/8 South Indian, 1/8 Indonesian. Ask me my race and I'll say Indian. But at the mosque, in NS and at college, people assume I'm Malay too, because of my religion, how I look and the Malay friends I'm with. In India, people guess I'm from somewhere else in India. My roots are in the South.

So who decides what I am: me, or the people looking? I asked five mixed-race friends the same question. Here are three of their stories, then all five voices together.

Part two · Learn

Who gets to decide?

The same person gets read differently depending on who is looking. Here are four places where someone else decides, and one where they decide for themselves.

Iqbal
Malay, with Javanese, Pakistani-Arab and Peranakan Chinese roots. Usually seen as Malay.
John
Indian father, Malay mother; grew up abroad. Usually seen as Chinese or Filipino.

1 · Strangers

John: older Grab drivers chat to him in Chinese. When he says he is Malay, they completely shut down.

Ryan, read as Malay, so assumed Muslim: …why are you eating pork or something?

Azri: seen as Indian, once I start opening my mouth… they… just assume I'm Malay.

Why do strangers feel they need to check?

Prosser (2001, p. 52): skin is a fantasmatic surface, a canvas for what we wish were true.

What this means: A race label tells strangers how to treat you: which language to use, what you eat, what to expect of you. Skin gives the first guess, and strangers fill in the rest with what they expect. When a name, a language or a habit doesn't fit, the picture breaks, so they check. Often a judgement comes with it, like …smart for someone who has a Malay side of the family.

2 · Community

Iqbal: There's no such thing as 100% Malay, lah, because every Malay is mixed in some way or form.

Kelly, told by Malay schoolmates: I will never belong as a Malay person.

Azri, among Tamil-speaking Indian Muslims: fully identifying alone.

Can you be left out of a community you share roots with?

Bhabha (1994) calls this the in-between: a liminal space where identity is negotiated.

What this means: Yes. Communities check for faith, language and looks before letting you in. Kelly is half Malay and Muslim, but she doesn't speak fluent Malay or look Malay, and found it hard to fit in. Schoolmates told her she'd never belong. Bhabha's in-between is not just a gap but it's where mixed people work out who they are, often alone.

3 · Family

Kelly: her Malay side is close. With her Chinese relatives: it feels as if they invited us for the sake of inviting us.

Azri: as a child, nobody spelled it out. My parents didn't explicitly tell me. So… it just felt like… just because I speak Malay, I'm Malay.

Ryan: his father played Tamil songs and gave him books on the Ramayana, but home is mostly English, and he can't speak Tamil.

What does a family pass down, and what does it leave out?

What this means: Families pass down language and faith, and these can outweigh looks. What gets left out, like Tamil for Ryan, becomes a gap others notice. Acceptance at home matters too. Kelly, kept at arm's length by her father's relatives, feels the in-between most. Iqbal, fully at home in Malay culture, feels no gap at all.

4 · Paper

Iqbal: his grandfather was an orphan who never knew his heritage. I guess they just put it as Malay.

John: his IC was changed to Malay-Indian for self-help group support (MENDAKI).

Azri: his IC says Indian, so no Malay tuition subsidy: I kind of just have to work twice as hard.

What does an IC decide that a face doesn't?

DeMello (2014, pp. 99–100): races are artificial categories.

What this means: The IC decides access: Mother Tongue, self-help groups, subsidies. It can be chosen (John), inherited by accident (Iqbal's grandfather), or a barrier (Azri). DeMello calls race a social, economic, and political system (p. 100). In Singapore, CMIO turns that system into paperwork: a way of sorting people, not a description of who they are.

5 · The self

Kelly: I've just said that I'm Chinese, in order to protect myself.

John: It's just easier, la. So if they say what race I am, I'll just say I'm Malay, la.

Azri: sometimes play the Indian card, sometimes play the Malay card.

Is choosing what to say freedom, or pressure?

Major & O'Brien (2005, p. 400): revealing a hidden part of yourself can raise identity threat.

What this means: Both. Saying one race can protect you (Kelly) or make life easier (John). But each time, part of you stays hidden. A short answer avoids the moment of explaining, which is exactly the moment Major & O'Brien say raises identity threat.

So who decides? Skin starts the sorting. Faith, language and paper decide whether it sticks. The person gets the last word, and often uses it to stay safe.
